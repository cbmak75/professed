The deep dive

Twelve declarations, one at a time.

The manifesto states the position in two minutes. This is the long version: an essay on each declaration, why I think it matters, and what it looks like on a Tuesday in practice. Each one is set against the rules that already bind solicitors and barristers in England and Wales, because the obvious objection to a manifesto like this is that it is special pleading, and the answer is that almost every declaration is an existing obligation restated.

Start at the beginning
I

A profession is a vow, not a job

The first of twelve: why the race to become AI-native keeps forgetting half the story, why the position is written down in public rather than carried around in practitioners' heads, and why everything that follows rests on the oldest meaning of the word profess. Read →

II

The machine can draft; a lawyer can profess

Advice is not a category of text. It comes into existence at the moment a person who has professed takes responsibility for it, which is what paragraph 3.5 of the SRA Code and rC20 of the BSB Handbook have been saying all along. Read →

III

Be AI-native where it saves time and money

A defence of the profession is not a defence of the profession's habits. What my first years in practice actually consisted of, why nothing in the codes protects slow or expensive, and why the client has to feel the benefit. Read →

IV

Be professed where judgement is sought

What would you do, in my position. The question clients ask at the end of a consultation, the four things it is really asking for, and why the boundary between routine work and judgement work is never marked on the file. Read →

V

Law is a permanent disagreement

Every reported case is a record of two qualified lawyers reading the same materials and reaching opposite conclusions. Why the properly arguable threshold cannot be met by the thing that wrote the contention, and why a better model will not close the gap. Read →

VI

Lawful and just are not the same word

Apartheid was law. Colonialism was administered through law, with the forms completed and the appeals available. Why a client facing a valid rule that produces an indefensible result is owed a conscience with something to lose. Read →

VII

The bottleneck is the point

The complaint arrives on cue in every conversation about supervision, and the observation behind it is correct. The conclusion is confused, because in law the human is not the checkpoint. The human is the product. Read →

VIII

Approval without engagement is counterfeit

It will not arrive as a scandal. It will arrive as a skim on a bad Thursday afternoon, and paragraph 1.4 of the SRA Code already calls that complicity by omission. Read →

IX

The burden of responsibility

There is a cruder test than anything else in the manifesto: ask who answers when the rules are broken. Run it on a solicitor, then run it on the machine, and notice that the asymmetry is not the kind of gap legislation closes. Read →

X

The client's protections are sacred

The declaration most likely to be breached by decent practitioners without anybody noticing. It will not arrive as a scandal; it will arrive as a paste, in the sincere service of the client, in four seconds. Read →

XI

The record proves the vow was kept

The machine has quietly destroyed the accidental audit trail that used to sit in every file. Careful practice and careless practice now leave the same mark, which is a new problem and not one solved by being conscientious. Read →

XII

Any firm can be AI-native; every firm must remain professed

Where the line runs, why almost nothing in the twelve declarations is original, and the one thought I would defend as more than a restatement: that the law has always known it is flawed, and said so out loud. Read →
